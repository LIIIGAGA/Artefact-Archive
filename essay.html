Spatial Storylets 空间叙事单元

Extending Ergodic Narrative into Three-Dimensional Space 将遍历性叙事延伸至三维空间

Author作者
Jiaying Li / 李佳颖
Tutor导师
Claude Dutson
Status状态
● Draft草稿

How can the design of a Spatial Storylet system transfer narrative grammar from temporal sequence into three-dimensional space?

Can this transfer enable fragmented narrative to achieve coherence without temporal sequence, and produce measurable effects on players' narrative meaning-making and emotional engagement?

空间叙事单元系统的设计如何将叙事语法从时间序列转化为三维空间？

这种转化能否使碎片化叙事在不依赖时间序列的情况下实现连贯性，并对玩家的叙事意义建构与情感投入产生可衡量的效果？

// Abstract// 摘要
Summary概述

In Espen Aarseth's conception of cybertext, "text" is understood broadly as a dynamic system encompassing content, rules, mechanisms, and user interaction, rather than merely as a static written sequence. Yet existing models of interactive narrative — including linear, branching, and ergodic models — continue to treat text as a product of temporal sequence.

This dissertation proposes an eighth variable — spatial morphology — to address the gap Aarseth's taxonomy leaves in the spatial dimension. Building on the storylets model, it introduces the concept of the Spatial Storylet: a narrative unit in which a text fragment is embedded within a designed spatial module, operating across three levels — the lexical layer, contextual layer, and grammatical layer.

A player's traversal of three-dimensional space is simultaneously an act of reading and an act of spatial production. Story is not stored in any single module; it emerges from the relational whole of the spatial configuration.

在Espen Aarseth的赛博文本理论中，"文本"被广泛理解为一个涵盖内容、规则、机制与用户交互的动态系统，而非仅仅是静态的书写序列。然而，现有的互动叙事模型——包括线性、分支与遍历性模型——仍将文本视为时间序列的产物。

本论文提出第八个变量——空间形态——以弥补Aarseth分类框架在空间维度上的缺口。在叙事单元模型的基础上，引入空间叙事单元的概念：一种将文本片段嵌入设计性空间模块的叙事单位，运作于三个层面——词汇层、语境层与语法层。

玩家对三维空间的遍历同时是一种阅读行为与空间生产行为。故事并不储存于任何单一模块之中，而是从空间配置的关系整体中涌现出来。

// Chapter 1// 第一章

Introduction引言

In the design practice of narrative video games, there has long existed a disconnect between space and text: the environment is neither shaped by the narrative content, nor does it shape the player's understanding of it. Players traverse carefully designed virtual spaces, yet they are often merely moving, not reading. 在叙事游戏的设计实践中，长期存在着空间与文本之间的断裂：环境既不被叙事内容所塑造，也不塑造玩家对叙事的理解。玩家穿越精心设计的虚拟空间，却往往只是在移动，而非在阅读。

Stories unfold through dialogue boxes and cutscenes, while space functions only as their backdrop. This disconnect is not a failure of individual designers, but a consequence of existing narrative models — when narrative coherence is understood as a product of temporal sequence, space can only serve as a container for time-based storytelling rather than as a source of meaning itself. 故事通过对话框和过场动画展开，而空间仅作为背景存在。这种断裂并非个别设计师的失败，而是现有叙事模型的必然结果——当叙事连贯性被理解为时间序列的产物时，空间只能充当时间性叙事的容器，而非意义本身的来源。

"The non-linearity of so-called non-linear narrative in text-based games is in fact an illusion. Even when no necessary causal relationship exists between events, the way in which each passage of text is perceived remains sequential.""文字游戏中所谓非线性叙事的非线性，实际上是一种幻觉。即使事件之间不存在必然的因果关系，每段文本被感知的方式依然是序列性的。"

In many ergodic text adventure games, space tends to exist only as a metaphorical element. In 80 Days, the narrative is structured as a complex, interwoven graph — but this topology remains too abstract, at the level of a two-dimensional diagram. The textual content is obviously more direct, naturally attracting more of the player's attention. 在许多遍历性文字冒险游戏中，空间往往仅作为隐喻性元素存在。在《80天》中，叙事被构建为一个复杂交织的图结构——但这种拓扑关系仍过于抽象，停留在二维图表的层面。文本内容显然更为直接，自然而然地吸引了玩家更多的注意力。

To achieve genuine non-linearity, we must turn to a medium of meaning that does not depend on temporal sequence — space. And space cannot be fully described. It can only be experienced. 要实现真正的非线性，我们必须转向一种不依赖时间序列的意义媒介——空间。而空间无法被完整描述，只能被体验。

A reliable solution is to use text to guarantee the volume and precision of narrative, and space to supply its grammar and structure — neither can be dispensed with. This tension leads directly to the central question:

Central Problem

How can spatial elements become an irreplaceable condition for the story to be understood, rather than an optional backdrop?

In the process of integrating textual and spatial narrative, how can narrative coherence be made to depend on spatial relations rather than temporal sequence?

// Chapter 2

Theoretical Framework

2.1 Linearity in Narrative Games

The main existing forms of interactive narrative structure — linear, branching, and ergodic — despite their significant differences in the degree of player autonomy, share a crucial common ground: story ultimately solidifies in the player's mind into a descriptive format, transitioning from a dynamic system into stable knowledge, and the way players perceive or reflect on it is essentially linear in nature.

Aarseth describes the "linearity" of hypertext as a topological concept: in a linear text, all nodes can only be accessed in a single fixed sequence, whereas a non-linear text contains forks and admits more than one possible path. However, the sense of "linear" discussed in this dissertation is more concerned with its two-dimensional character in contrast to three-dimensional spatiality — both interpretations can be summarised as flat diagrammatic representations rather than as spaces that can be traversed by a body.

2.2 Aarseth's Ergodic Theory and the Seven-Variable Framework

2.2.1 Text & Cybertext

In Cybertext, Aarseth poses the question: in what sense are hypertext, adventure games, and similar forms to be considered text at all? The reader of a cybertext is placed in an insecure position: every choice made represents a possible path that refuses to be traversed.

"The reader's subjective effort is engaged in generating the world itself, rather than merely introducing a personal interpretive perspective onto a text whose existence proceeds undisturbed. The reader's struggle is to obtain 'the story as mine', which is essentially a form of personal improvisation."

— Aarseth, Cybertext (1997), p.4

2.2.2 The Seven-Variable Framework

Central to Aarseth's framework is the distinction between textons and scriptons, mediated by the traversal function. To systematise the analysis of ergodic texts, Aarseth proposes seven variables:

# Variable Levels What it describes
1DynamicsStatic / IDT / TDTWhether textual content changes
2DeterminabilityDeterminate / IndeterminateWhether traversal function yields consistent outputs
3TransiencyTransient / IntransientWhether content advances automatically with time
4PerspectivePersonal / ImpersonalWhether user has a role within the world
5AccessRandom / ControlledWhether all content is reachable at any time
6LinkingExplicit / Conditional / NoneHow connections between nodes operate
7User FunctionInterpretive / Explorative / Configurative / TextonicWhat the user can do beyond interpreting

We can observe that "spatiality" appears twice among these seven variables, yet in both instances only as an abstract condition. In the Access variable, location operates merely as a logical label. In the Linking variable, "adjacency" remains adjacency in the topological sense, not in terms of actual spatial coordinates. Aarseth's seven-variable framework therefore describes the logical and procedural dimensions of ergodic texts with considerable precision — yet in the ergodic texts he examines, space is invariably logical and representational.

2.3 The Eighth Variable: Spatial Morphology

This dissertation proposes an eighth variable — spatial morphology — to extend Aarseth's classificatory system from the abstract two-dimensional domain into three-dimensional space. Spatial morphology is divided into three levels:

Level 01
Decorative

Spatial form is present but exerts no structural influence on narrative meaning. Space functions only as visual backdrop. A player may entirely disregard spatial form without any loss of comprehension.

Level 02
Contextual

Spatial form modifies and inflects the interpretation of narrative content, but is not a necessary condition for understanding the story. Space functions as an enhancer of narrative, not as its grammar.

Level 03
Constitutive

Spatial form is a necessary condition for narrative meaning. Without spatial configuration, the story does not cohere — textual fragments cannot be integrated, and meaning cannot emerge.

2.4 Storylet Theory

The term storylet originated in the game Fallen London, developed by Failbetter Games. Emily Short defines it as "a way of organizing narrative content with more flexibility than the typical branching narrative." Each storylet consists of three elements: narrative content; a set of preconditions; and a set of effects.

Yet in the classificatory framework of Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin, space appears in only an extremely limited capacity, serving merely as one type of precondition. Location functions solely as a logical switch: the player arrives at a specific place, the spatial condition is satisfied, and narrative content is triggered. Two spaces both tagged as "library" will trigger identical storylets regardless of how great the formal differences between them may be.

2.5 Spatial Storylet: Definition and Three-Layer Model

This dissertation proposes the Spatial Storylet model, which introduces a fourth element — the spatial module — alongside the three basic components of the storylet. The same passage of text, when embedded within spatial modules of different forms, produces different narrative meanings.

Example

Take a passage about "waiting." If embedded within a broad, open hall, the expansiveness inflects "waiting" as solitude or abandonment; if the same text is embedded within a narrow, enclosed corridor, the oppressiveness inflects "waiting" as anxiety or entrapment.

The production of narrative meaning operates across three levels:

Lexical Layer

The fixed textual fragments contained within each module. Their wording does not change regardless of how the player traverses the system. Precisely because the text is stable, any variation in meaning must arise from the spatial and relational context in which it is situated.

Contextual Layer

The modification of text by spatial form — encompassing scale, enclosure, orientation, material, and the module's relationships to adjacent spaces. A unified design language and the recurrence of spatial motifs enable semantic intertextuality between modules.

Grammatical Layer

The rules governing spatial connection between modules: which modules are adjacent, in what sequence they may be traversed, and the logic by which overall configuration changes in response to player action. Spatial relationships realise a kind of narrative spatial grammar.

"A cybertext is a machine for the production of variety of expression — in the present model, spatial configuration is precisely the variety of expression the text produces."

— Aarseth, Cybertext (1997), p.3
// Chapter 3

Case Analysis

This chapter applies the analytical framework developed in the preceding chapters to three existing narrative games, with particular attention to the degree to which each realises a relationship between spatial morphology and narrative meaning.

Disco Elysium — ZA/UM, 2019
Decorative → Contextual

Can be understood as a form of visual novel: the core gameplay experience is the reading of text, structured around dice-check mechanics. The environment is a two-dimensional illustration disguised as three-dimensional space, owing to the fixed top-down camera perspective.

In most scenes, such traversal function is logical in nature, not spatial — demonstrating purely decorative spatial morphology. However, in certain sequences where text is tied to specific objects rather than providing global environmental information, the environment shifts toward contextual spatial morphology: non-textual narrative cues connect fragmentary textual information into a coherent, intelligible whole.

Citizen Sleeper — Jump Over the Age, 2022
Contextual

The player takes on the role of a "Sleeper" stranded on an abandoned space station. The various zones are not merely narrative backdrops — they are functional. Each zone simultaneously carries narrative content, a resource system, and a network of character relationships.

Yet the spatial configuration remains predetermined and fixed. The player will still wake in the same place, still obtain supplies in the same locations. Space begins to carry narrative units — but the narrative can stand independently of spatial form.

Blue Prince — Dogubomb, 2025
Constitutive Attempt — Disconnected

The spatial configuration changes according to the player's choices, with procedural generation enabling dynamic reconfiguration. Yet a disconnection of gameplay from narrative becomes apparent: once the player has fully mastered the system, the rooms become purely instrumental. The recombination logic bears no relationship to narrative meaning.

Although the spatial configuration is recombined with each traversal — achieving a very high degree of freedom — the game mechanics do not serve the narrative in any productive way.

// Chapter 4

Design Practice

This chapter presents an original game design — Osaider, a modular palace whose spatial configuration dynamically reconfigures in response to player input — as a practical exploration of the Spatial Storylet system.

Two prototype versions are developed for comparative study: a text-only narrative prototype and a full spatial prototype. Player experience data from both versions will be used to evaluate whether narrative spatial grammar produces effective meaning-making and heightened emotional engagement.
